Dashed outlines on the ground are building capture areas. Each meter shows how much of that area the wash covers; at 70% the building unwinds into its whimsical form (pick the effect above, or press Replay).

Notes: palette and mechanic

  • Joy changes things, not just colour. Iron panels, pipes and gauges become curved tile roofs, window boxes and lanterns; smokestack steam turns into drifting notes, paint swirls and petals. Where the wash edge is passing, you catch in-between states: a gear unwinding into a flower, a pipe sprouting a vine, steam half-turned into notes.
  • Industrial clutter, with a joyful twin for each piece. Pipe bridges become vine-wrapped arches, exhaust fans become pinwheels, louvered vents become birdhouses, floor grates become flower beds, gas tanks become flower barrels, the boiler becomes a kettle tea stall, and the hazard-stripe kerb becomes a flower border.
  • People transform too. Grey townsfolk are plainly human but drilled: caps and long drab coats, upright arms-in-tight posture, pocket-watch checks, and a faint dotted route they keep walking. Joy loosens them into relaxed or dancing poses in affinity colours (music dusty blue, art warm rose, nature sage) with playful hats.
  • Capture by land. Each building owns a dashed footprint on the ground. Its meter is the fraction of that footprint covered by the actual wash mask, measured per pixel. Below 70% the building stays mechanical (painted parts pick up colour); at 70% it switches wholesale to the whimsical version, with a brief mechanical unwinding (steam released as notes and petals, or iron panels folding open).
  • Edge treatment is presentation only. Brush strokes, vines and staff lines are three different ways to dress the same mask boundary, so it can follow the affinity or the zone type later.
